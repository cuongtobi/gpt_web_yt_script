# Story Spine — How Humans Domesticated Cannabis

## Central question

**How did humans turn an ancestral Asian cannabis population into the very different fiber, seed, and drug crops we recognize today?**

## Opening

Open on the **wooden braziers from Jirzankal Cemetery in the Pamirs**, roughly 2,500 years old. Chemical residues show that cannabis was burned in a mortuary context and the cannabinoid signature is consistent with relatively high-THC material.

Use the contrast immediately: this is vivid evidence for psychoactive use, but it is **not the beginning** of cannabis domestication. By that point humans had likely been managing and moving the plant for thousands of years.

Central question appears within the first minute.

## Beat 1 — Domestication is a population process, not a single discovery

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
Define domestication as repeated planting, harvesting, seed saving, movement, and differential reproduction. Cannabis is wind-pollinated, commonly dioecious, and capable of gene flow with feral populations.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
It prevents the misleading “one inventor, one place, one purpose” story and explains why wild/feral labels are messy.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
If geography alone cannot identify the ancestor, genomics becomes the next tool.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Cannabis reproductive biology; crop/feral gene flow; general domestication mechanism.

## Beat 2 — Genomes move the origin toward East Asia

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
The 2021 whole-genome study of more than 100 accessions found a basal/ancestral group in East Asia and inferred an early-Neolithic domestication history, on the order of ~12,000 years ago.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
It provides the strongest modern evidence for where and roughly when the sustained cultivation relationship began.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
A very early domestication date raises the obvious question: what were people selecting cannabis for before specialized modern drug crops existed?

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Ren et al., 2021 *Science Advances*.

## Beat 3 — One plant, several useful products

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
Cannabis offered bast fiber, nutritious/oil-bearing seed, and variable chemistry in flowering tissues. Early use could be multipurpose; archaeology rarely gives a clean single-purpose origin.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
Multipurpose utility explains why the relationship could begin before strong specialization and why multiple selection paths later emerged.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
Repeated selection for different outputs eventually creates trade-offs in plant architecture and chemistry.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Cannabis morphology/ethnobotany; archaeobotanical caution.

## Beat 4 — Human preferences split the crop

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
The 2021 genomic study estimates a major divergence between hemp/fiber and drug-type lineages around ~4,000 years ago. Fiber systems reward long stems and bast tissue; resin-focused systems reward flower production, branching, trichomes, and cannabinoid chemistry.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
This is the core mechanism of domestication: human goals become evolutionary pressure and leave a genomic signature.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
Once people have useful specialized crops, they carry them outward.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Ren 2021; Sawler 2015; plant morphology and cannabinoid genetics.

## Beat 5 — Movement creates local experiments

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
Cannabis spreads broadly through Eurasia. New latitudes, photoperiods, climates, markets, and farming practices filter which plants succeed. Local seed saving creates landraces.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
Domestication is not only selection at the origin; movement repeatedly reshapes the crop.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
Regional selection can emphasize chemistry as well as fiber or seed, setting up the clearest archaeological psychoactive case.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Long et al. archaeobotanical synthesis; photoperiod biology.

## Beat 6 — Jirzankal proves use, not a first invention

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
Around 500 BCE, cannabis was burned in wooden braziers at Jirzankal. Residues are consistent with cannabis containing relatively high THC compared with typical wild expectations.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
It gives a concrete snapshot of humans valuing cannabis chemistry, while demonstrating the limits of archaeological inference.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
To understand how such traits become stable crops, explain selection on reproduction and chemistry.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Ren et al., 2019 *Science Advances*.

## Beat 7 — Selection works without knowing genetics

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
Saving seed from plants with desirable stem, seed, flower, or chemical traits changes the odds in the next generation. Gene flow, crossing, and feralization keep boundaries porous; chemistry and architecture are selectable but not perfectly controlled.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
This is the simple causal engine connecting ancient farmer behavior to later genetic population structure.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
Modern breeding uses the same mechanism with much more precise tools.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Sawler 2015; van Bakel 2011; Ren 2021; general breeding biology.

## Beat 8 — Domestication never stopped

**What happened / what do we learn?**  
Modern breeders select industrial hemp, oilseed, CBD-rich, and high-THC crops; use controlled crosses, sex management, clones, and measured chemistry; legal regimes can also shape breeding targets.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**  
The modern diversity of cannabis is the latest layer of the same human-driven evolutionary process, not a separate story.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**  
Direct answer and callback.

**Evidence / cases used:**  
Modern breeding/genomic literature; no exact market or legal threshold claims.

## Evidence placement

- Opening/payoff: Jirzankal braziers.
- Origin: Ren 2021 genome study.
- Specialization: Ren 2021 + Sawler 2015.
- Mechanism: stem vs flower architecture, photoperiod, cannabinoid biosynthesis.
- Spread: archaeobotanical synthesis.
- Modern form: clone/cross/chemotype breeding as continuation.

## Ending

**Direct answer:** Humans domesticated cannabis through thousands of years of repeated planting, movement, seed saving, crossing, and selection for different useful traits. It was not one event or one purpose.

**Synthesis:** ancestral East Asian gene pool → early multipurpose cultivation → gradual specialization → hemp/drug population divergence → Eurasian movement and local landraces → ritual/psychoactive evidence → modern precision breeding.

**Callback:** Return to the Pamir braziers: they are not the beginning, but one mid-story snapshot showing that by 2,500 years ago humans were already exploiting a chemical dimension of a plant they had been reshaping for millennia.

## Story Spine readiness

**PASS.** The beats form a causal/chronological transformation; swapping several beats would break the logic because origin precedes multipurpose utility, specialization creates transportable crop forms, movement creates regional selection, and modern breeding is the endpoint of the same mechanism.
