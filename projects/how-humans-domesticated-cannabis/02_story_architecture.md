## Tentative viewer promise

Understand why Cannabis domestication is better approached as a sequence of human selection pressures than as a single dramatic “discovery,” while learning which evidence can actually support claims about origin, use, and divergence.

## Tentative opening engine

A split-screen object contrast: strong plant fiber on one side, a dense resinous flower on the other. Both came from the same species-level lineage, but they embody very different human goals. That visual leads directly to the central question: how did human selection create such different plants, and can we reconstruct where that process began?

## Tentative causal ladder — requires verified claim IDs

1. People encounter/use a useful wild plant → repeated harvesting creates opportunity for deliberate tending or cultivation. `[C01/C03 — UNVERIFIED]`
2. Cultivation makes plant-to-plant differences visible and selectable → people preferentially propagate traits that match local uses. `[C04 — UNVERIFIED]`
3. Different uses reward different trait bundles → selection trajectories begin to diverge. `[C01/C04 — UNVERIFIED]`
4. Cultivated plants move with people and cross with other populations → simple one-place/one-time origin stories become harder to sustain. `[C02/C06 — UNVERIFIED]`
5. Archaeological finds document particular kinds of use, but not all finds prove domestication. `[C03/C05 — UNVERIFIED]`
6. Genomic evidence can test relationships among modern lineages, but inference depends on sampling and model assumptions. `[C02/C06 — UNVERIFIED]`
7. The best answer may be a layered process: early cultivation, divergent selection for multiple uses, movement/admixture, and later intensive breeding. `[ALL — UNVERIFIED]`

## Tentative macro open loop

If hemp-like plants and drug-type plants look and behave so differently, what sequence of human choices produced both—and does the evidence point to one beginning or many layers of domestication?

## Tentative payoffs

1. Early payoff: domestication is selection across generations, not merely “people found a useful plant.”
2. Mid payoff: evidence of use does not automatically equal evidence of domestication.
3. Major reframe: modern populations may not preserve a clean snapshot of ancestral wild Cannabis.
4. Final payoff: the history is likely a process reconstructed from overlapping evidence types, not a single origin anecdote.
