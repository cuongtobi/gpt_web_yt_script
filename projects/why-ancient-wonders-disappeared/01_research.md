## 1. Central question candidates

1. Vì sao sáu trong Bảy Kỳ quan Thế giới Cổ đại biến mất, trong khi Đại Kim tự tháp Giza vẫn còn?
2. Một công trình khổng lồ cần những điều kiện gì để sống sót qua hàng nghìn năm — và vì sao phần lớn kỳ quan cổ đại không có được những điều kiện đó?

## 2. Timeline / causal background

- Thời Hellenistic: truyền thống các danh sách “những công trình đáng xem” của thế giới Hy Lạp–Địa Trung Hải định hình khái niệm Bảy Kỳ quan.
- Thế kỷ 4 TCN: Đền Artemis ở Ephesus bị cháy năm 356 TCN rồi được xây lại.
- Khoảng đầu thế kỷ 3 TCN: Colossus of Rhodes được dựng; khoảng 226 TCN bị động đất làm đổ.
- Thời Ptolemaic: Lighthouse of Alexandria được xây trên đảo Pharos; tồn tại qua nhiều thế kỷ, sau đó chịu nhiều đợt động đất và suy thoái.
- Thời cổ đại muộn: các hệ thống tôn giáo/chính trị quanh Artemis và Zeus thay đổi; bảo trì và chức năng nghi lễ suy giảm.
- Trung Cổ: Mausoleum at Halicarnassus và Lighthouse of Alexandria tiếp tục hư hại; vật liệu từ phế tích được tái sử dụng trong công trình phòng thủ về sau.
- Hiện đại: Great Pyramid of Giza vẫn là kỳ quan duy nhất còn đứng ở quy mô lớn; Hanging Gardens vẫn chưa được xác định khảo cổ một cách dứt khoát.

## 3. Core claims

### C01
- Claim: “Bảy Kỳ quan” là truyền thống danh sách của tác giả Hy Lạp/Hellenistic, không phải danh mục toàn cầu về bảy công trình lớn nhất thế giới cổ đại.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Antipater of Sidon tradition; Peter A. Clayton & Martin J. Price (eds.), *The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World* (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: surviving Greek literary tradition presents a travel-oriented canon concentrated in the Mediterranean/Near East.
- Limits / qualification: exact canonical list and authorship varied across antiquity before the later familiar seven became standard.

### C02
- Claim: Đại Kim tự tháp Giza là kỳ quan duy nhất trong danh sách cổ điển còn tồn tại ở quy mô kiến trúc lớn.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Mark Lehner, *The Complete Pyramids* (1997); Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: Great Pyramid remains standing at Giza while the other six are lost or survive only as ruins/archaeological traces.
- Limits / qualification: pyramid has lost much of its outer casing and has been altered/robbed over time.

### C03
- Claim: Colossus of Rhodes là tượng đồng khổng lồ, cao hơn 30 m theo truyền thống phổ biến, được dựng đầu thế kỷ 3 TCN và bị động đất làm đổ khoảng 226 TCN.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 34; Strabo, *Geography* 14; Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: ancient sources describe its exceptional scale and earthquake destruction.
- Limits / qualification: dimensions and reconstruction details vary; do not claim a precise engineering scheme not supported by sources.

### C04
- Claim: Phần Colossus bị đổ vẫn là vật đáng xem trong nhiều thế kỷ; câu chuyện kim loại bị bán sau cuộc chinh phục Arab là truyền thống muộn và không chắc chắn.
- Status: SUPPORTED / DEBATED
- Source: Pliny the Elder for fallen remains; later Byzantine/medieval tradition for removal story; modern discussion in Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: ancient testimony supports long-lived ruins; the final removal narrative is much later.
- Limits / qualification: final disappearance should be narrated as uncertain.

### C05
- Claim: Lighthouse of Alexandria stood on Pharos, survived for many centuries, and was progressively damaged by earthquakes before disappearing in the late medieval period.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: ancient descriptions including Strabo; modern synthesis in Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: repeated historical testimony records the lighthouse and later damage; medieval earthquakes are central to its decline.
- Limits / qualification: avoid over-precise single-event destruction story.

### C06
- Claim: Citadel of Qaitbay was built at/near the former Pharos site in the 15th century, with material from the older ruins commonly understood to have been reused.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: architectural/archaeological summaries in modern studies of Alexandria and Pharos; Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: citadel occupies the former lighthouse location and incorporates ancient masonry/spolia.
- Limits / qualification: phrase as “được cho là / có vật liệu tái sử dụng” rather than asserting every stone came from the lighthouse.

### C07
- Claim: Mausoleum at Halicarnassus survived for centuries, was damaged by earthquakes, and its stone was later reused by the Knights of St. John in Bodrum fortifications.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: archaeological history of Halicarnassus; Clayton & Price (1988); British Museum scholarship on Mausoleum sculptures/excavations.
- Support / exact evidence: medieval reuse of masonry in the castle is documented in site history.
- Limits / qualification: collapse was a process, not one securely dated final earthquake.

### C08
- Claim: Temple of Artemis at Ephesus was destroyed by fire in 356 TCN and rebuilt; its later disappearance involved multiple phases of damage, religious change, urban decline and reuse rather than one securely documented “final destruction” event.
- Status: ESTABLISHED for 356 TCN fire/rebuilding; SUPPORTED for multi-stage later decline.
- Source: ancient tradition including Strabo/Pliny; archaeological history of Ephesus; Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: the sanctuary had successive building phases and remained important after the 4th-century TCN fire.
- Limits / qualification: later Gothic attack and Christian-era destruction are often simplified; avoid claiming a single final culprit.

### C09
- Claim: Statue of Zeus at Olympia was chryselephantine — gold and ivory over an internal structure — rather than a solid stone monolith.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Pausanias, *Description of Greece* Book 5; ancient descriptions summarized in Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: ancient descriptions specify gold, ivory and structural components.
- Limits / qualification: exact construction details are reconstructed from literary evidence.

### C10
- Claim: The final fate of the Statue of Zeus is uncertain; the tradition that it was moved to Constantinople and destroyed by fire should not be treated as fully secure.
- Status: DEBATED
- Source: late antique/Byzantine traditions; modern syntheses in Clayton & Price (1988).
- Support / exact evidence: competing traditions exist and direct evidence is limited.
- Limits / qualification: final narration must explicitly preserve uncertainty.

### C11
- Claim: The Hanging Gardens of Babylon have not been conclusively identified archaeologically at Babylon.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Diodorus Siculus and other classical descriptions; Stephanie Dalley, *The Mystery of the Hanging Garden of Babylon* (2013).
- Support / exact evidence: literary descriptions survive, but a universally accepted archaeological identification at Babylon does not.
- Limits / qualification: absence of definitive identification is not proof the gardens never existed.

### C12
- Claim: A major modern hypothesis places the tradition behind the Hanging Gardens at Nineveh rather than Babylon.
- Status: DEBATED
- Source: Stephanie Dalley (2013).
- Support / exact evidence: Dalley argues Assyrian textual/archaeological evidence better matches the garden tradition.
- Limits / qualification: this is a scholarly hypothesis, not consensus fact.

### C13
- Claim: Reuse of ancient building material (spolia) was common in later cities; ruined monuments could become convenient sources of pre-cut stone and metal.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: broad archaeological scholarship on spolia and reuse in the Roman/late antique/medieval Mediterranean; site histories of Halicarnassus and Alexandria.
- Support / exact evidence: reused architectural blocks and sculpture are widely documented across ancient/medieval sites.
- Limits / qualification: do not imply every lost monument was systematically quarried in the same way.

### C14
- Claim: Large monuments require continued maintenance, institutional support and social function; loss of these systems accelerates deterioration and reuse.
- Status: INTERPRETATION grounded in architectural history
- Source: synthesis from the case histories above.
- Support / exact evidence: temples, statues, towers and civic monuments all depend on repair, security and funding.
- Limits / qualification: this is explanatory synthesis, not a single-source causal law.

### C15
- Claim: Great Pyramid’s survival is plausibly helped by its enormous mass, broad pyramidal geometry, stone construction and dry environment.
- Status: SUPPORTED / INTERPRETATION
- Source: Mark Lehner (1997) for construction/material context; comparative architectural reasoning.
- Support / exact evidence: huge masonry mass and low center of gravity contrast with tall statues, towers and roofed temples.
- Limits / qualification: do not claim these are the only reasons or quantify survival probability.

### C16
- Claim: The most accurate general explanation is cumulative: disaster can create ruins, while loss of maintenance, political/religious change and material reuse determine whether ruins remain recognizable.
- Status: INTERPRETATION
- Source: synthesis of C03–C15.
- Support / exact evidence: different wonders show different combinations of earthquake, fire, institutional decline and reuse.
- Limits / qualification: each wonder has a distinct history; avoid claiming one universal destruction mechanism.

## 4. Strong cases / objects / studies

1. **Great Pyramid of Giza** — surviving control case; shows how mass, geometry, stone and environment can matter.
2. **Colossus of Rhodes** — short standing life followed by centuries as ruins; strongest example that “destroyed” ≠ “instantly vanished.”
3. **Lighthouse of Alexandria** — repeated earthquake damage plus later rebuilding/reuse at the same strategic site.
4. **Mausoleum at Halicarnassus** — long survival, earthquake damage, then documented stone reuse in Bodrum fortifications.
5. **Temple of Artemis** — repeated destruction/rebuilding and later loss of social/religious function.
6. **Statue of Zeus** — material/construction vulnerability plus unusually uncertain final fate.
7. **Hanging Gardens** — strongest reminder that literary fame and archaeological certainty are not the same thing.

## 5. Useful numbers and quotes

- 226 TCN: conventional date for the earthquake that toppled the Colossus.
- 356 TCN: conventional date of the famous fire at the Temple of Artemis.
- Colossus: generally described as a little over 30 m tall; use “hơn ba mươi mét” rather than a false-precision figure.
- 15th century: safe broad date for construction of Qaitbay Citadel and Knights’ reuse of Halicarnassus masonry.
- No direct quotation is necessary for the final script; avoid quote-verification risk.

## 6. Important uncertainties

1. The exact final removal/disposal of the fallen Colossus is uncertain; the scrap-sale story is late.
2. The final fate of the Statue of Zeus is uncertain; Constantinople/fire tradition should be qualified.
3. Hanging Gardens have no universally accepted archaeological identification at Babylon; Nineveh is a debated hypothesis.
4. Temple of Artemis’s late-antique end was multi-stage; avoid a single-cause “destroyed by X” claim.
5. “Why the Great Pyramid survived” is comparative interpretation, not a single proven causal experiment.
6. Spolia/reuse is a common pattern, but not every wonder disappeared by quarrying.
