# Hook Lab — How Did Humans Domesticate Cannabis?

### H1 — Contradiction

**Hook**
Cannabis may be one of humanity’s oldest domesticated plants. But the plant people first cultivated was almost certainly not the same thing as the modern crop divided into industrial hemp and high-potency drug varieties. For thousands of years, humans were changing cannabis before they had any idea what THC, CBD, or even genes were. The strange part is that the plant’s genome still records those choices.

**Bridge**
This leads naturally into the genomic evidence showing an early multipurpose crop that later split into specialized breeding trajectories.

### H2 — Concrete scene

**Hook**
In a cemetery high in the eastern Pamirs, archaeologists found wooden braziers containing chemical traces of cannabis burned about 2,500 years ago. The residues suggest plants with unusually strong psychoactive chemistry. Yet by the time those braziers were used, humans had already been living with and reshaping cannabis for thousands of years. That gap is where the real domestication story sits.

**Evidence anchors**
- C11, C12, C3, Case D

**Bridge**
The story can move backward from a securely dated psychoactive use to the much older cultivation history that produced such different forms.

### H3 — Mystery / evidence first

**Hook**
When researchers compared more than a hundred cannabis genomes from around the world, the plants did not point cleanly to the place many people expected. Instead of a simple Central Asian origin story, the deepest domestication signal clustered around East Asian landraces and feral plants. Even stranger, the truly wild ancestor may no longer be identifiable at all. Humans may have altered cannabis so thoroughly that its starting point has partly disappeared.

**Evidence anchors**
- C2, C4, C14, Case A, Case F

**Bridge**
This opens into the problem of reconstructing domestication when archaeology, genomics, and feral plants preserve different pieces of the past.

### H4 — Reverse assumption

**Hook**
Cannabis was probably not domesticated because ancient people wanted a stronger drug. The evidence points to something broader: an early crop valuable for several things at once, including fiber and seed, with highly specialized drug and hemp forms emerging much later. What looks today like one plant with one famous use began as a much messier relationship between humans and a remarkably versatile species.

**Evidence anchors**
- C5, C6, C15, C16

**Bridge**
This leads directly into the multipurpose-crop hypothesis and the later divergence caused by different human preferences.

### H5 — Mechanism in motion

**Hook**
Every season, farmers make a genetic decision even when they never see a chromosome. Save seed from the tallest stalks, and the next generation leans a little more toward fiber. Keep plants whose flowers produce stronger effects, and another set of traits becomes more common. Repeat that process for centuries, move the plants into new environments, and one ancestral population can be pulled into radically different forms.

**Evidence anchors**
- C6, C7, C8, C16, Case E

**Bridge**
This mechanism provides the causal engine for the entire documentary: repeated selection turns human preferences into plant biology.

### H6 — Before → after transformation

**Hook**
At one end of cannabis history is an early multipurpose plant, useful enough to keep close but not yet split into the categories we recognize today. At the other end are tall fiber crops bred for long stems and chemically potent plants bred for resin-rich flowers. Between those two states lies roughly twelve thousand years of cultivation, movement, escape, hybridization, and selection.

**Evidence anchors**
- C3, C5, C6, C7, C8

**Bridge**
The documentary can then reconstruct the major steps that transformed a generalist crop into specialized lineages.

### H7 — Object hook

**Hook**
A cannabis seed is tiny, but archaeologists can use its size and shape to ask a surprisingly large question: what were ancient farmers selecting this plant to do? At sites such as Haimenkou in southwest China, hundreds of preserved grains suggest cannabis was not standing still. The crop itself was changing as people pushed it toward different uses.

**Evidence anchors**
- C9, Case B

**Bridge**
The seed becomes an entry point into how physical archaeological remains can reveal domestication before written breeding records exist.

### H8 — Stakes hook

**Hook**
If we reduce cannabis history to a story about intoxication, we miss why humans kept moving this plant across continents for millennia. Fiber could become cord and cloth. Seeds could become food and oil. Chemical traits could be preserved for medicine, ritual, or intoxication. Its survival as a human companion depended on being useful in more than one way—and those competing uses eventually pulled the species apart.

**Evidence anchors**
- C5, C15, C6

**Bridge**
This sets up utility as the reason cannabis stayed under human selection long enough to diversify so dramatically.

### H9 — Timeline jump

**Hook**
Around 500 BCE, people in the Pamir Mountains were burning psychoactive cannabis in wooden braziers. More than ten thousand years earlier, according to one genomic reconstruction, the ancestors of cultivated cannabis were already beginning to diverge from a basal East Asian population. Put those two moments together and the familiar story flips: psychoactive cannabis is not the beginning of domestication. It is one late branch of a much older experiment.

**Evidence anchors**
- C3, C11, C12, Case A, Case D

**Bridge**
This lets the story jump from a vivid late archaeological case back to the deep domestication timeline.

### H10 — Unexpected cause

**Hook**
The biggest force that transformed cannabis may have been something completely ordinary: people repeatedly saving the plants that worked best for the job in front of them. Not genetic engineering. Not knowledge of cannabinoids. Just selection—fiber from one kind of plant, desirable seeds from another, stronger chemical effects from another. Over enough generations, practical choices became separate biological trajectories.

**Evidence anchors**
- C6, C7, C8, C16

**Bridge**
This opens a causal story centered on unconscious and conscious selection rather than a single discovery event.
