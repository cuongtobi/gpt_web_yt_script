# Research Pack — How Did Humans Domesticate Cannabis?

## 1. Central question candidates

1. How did repeated human use and selection turn ancestral cannabis into a crop with radically different fiber, seed, and psychoactive forms?
2. What does genetics plus archaeology actually let us say about where, when, and why cannabis became domesticated?

## 2. Timeline / causal background

- Early Holocene / early Neolithic: genomic modeling published in 2021 is consistent with early domestication in East Asia, with divergence from a basal gene pool dated to roughly 12,000 years before present, though the interval is broad and the exact wild progenitor remains uncertain.
- Early use: archaeological evidence indicates cannabis was used in East Asia for seeds/fiber and likely as a multipurpose crop long before securely documented psychoactive smoking.
- By roughly 4,000 years before present: genomic reconstruction suggests lineages leading to hemp-type and drug-type cannabis had strongly diverged, consistent with sustained selection toward different end uses.
- Bronze/Iron Age: cannabis remains and textiles become more widespread across Eurasia. Morphometric work on archaeological seeds indicates diversification in cultivated forms and uses.
- ca. 500 BCE: chemical residues in wooden braziers from Jirzankal Cemetery in the eastern Pamirs provide directly dated evidence of cannabis with high psychoactive compound levels being burned in mortuary contexts.
- Historical period onward: humans moved fiber- and drug-associated forms across Eurasia, Africa, and eventually the Americas; later breeding intensified chemical and morphological specialization.

## 3. Core claims

### C1
- Claim: Cannabis is among the oldest domesticated crop plants.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al., Science Advances (2021), doi:10.1126/sciadv.abg2286; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284894/
- Support / exact evidence: Whole-genome resequencing of 110 accessions reconstructed an early domestication history extending back to the early Neolithic.
- Limits / qualification: “One of the oldest” is safer than asserting a precise rank among all crops.

### C2
- Claim: A 2021 genomic study supports a single major domestication origin in East Asia rather than the older default assumption of Central Asia.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021), Science Advances; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8284894/
- Support / exact evidence: Population genomic analyses grouped basal landraces/feral plants from China as sister to hemp and drug cultivars and modeled domestication in East Asia.
- Limits / qualification: This is a major genomic reconstruction, not the final word on the species’ natural geographic origin; later work still describes aspects of origin/domestication as debated.

### C3
- Claim: The genomic split between the basal gene pool and early domesticated ancestors was modeled at about 12,000 years before present.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021).
- Support / exact evidence: Approximate modeled divergence ~12,000 BP with a reported 95% interval of 6,458–15,728 years BP.
- Limits / qualification: This is a demographic-model estimate, not an archaeological date marking a single domestication event.

### C4
- Claim: Pure wild progenitors of modern Cannabis sativa may no longer survive in an easily identifiable form.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021); review literature including https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9104644/
- Support / exact evidence: The 2021 analysis found sampled “feral” plants nested with domesticated groups and suggested true wild progenitors may be extinct.
- Limits / qualification: Later pangenome work notes that wild relatives in Asia may still exist; phrase as unresolved rather than absolute.

### C5
- Claim: Early cannabis was probably a multipurpose crop rather than being domesticated solely for intoxication.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021); Stevens et al./Fuller-related archaeobotanical synthesis reflected in https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127845/
- Support / exact evidence: Genomic study proposes multipurpose use until ~4,000 BP; archaeobotanical record includes seeds and fiber-related evidence.
- Limits / qualification: “Probably” and “proposes” should be retained; early specific uses are unevenly documented.

### C6
- Claim: Hemp-type and drug-type lineages underwent strong divergent selection, with different morphological and biochemical targets.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021).
- Support / exact evidence: Genome-wide differentiation plus selection signals in genes associated with branching, cellulose/lignin biosynthesis, and cannabinoid pathways.
- Limits / qualification: Modern categories simplify a continuum of landraces and chemotypes.

### C7
- Claim: Selection for fiber favored plant architecture and cell-wall traits different from selection for drug production.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021).
- Support / exact evidence: Candidate genes under divergent selection include branching-pattern and cellulose/lignin-related loci.
- Limits / qualification: Do not imply every trait was consciously selected one-by-one; much selection can be indirect.

### C8
- Claim: Human selection also reshaped cannabinoid synthase genes associated with THC- and CBD-dominant chemotypes.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021).
- Support / exact evidence: Study identified loss-of-function patterns in genes involved in synthesis of competing major cannabinoids under different breeding trajectories.
- Limits / qualification: Ancient cultivators did not know the molecules or genes; selection acted on observable plant effects and properties.

### C9
- Claim: Archaeological cannabis seeds can help reveal changing cultivation and crop specialization.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Fuller et al.-associated morphometric study (2024), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127845/
- Support / exact evidence: Measurements of >800 grains from Haimenkou were compared with modern forms; authors interpret the assemblage as a crop undergoing diversification toward alternative uses.
- Limits / qualification: Seed-size ranges overlap; morphometrics cannot cleanly label every ancient seed as fiber or psychoactive type.

### C10
- Claim: Securely identified ancient hemp fiber evidence is later and less straightforward than popular “12,000-year-old hemp textile” claims often imply.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Morphometric/archaeobotanical review, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127845/
- Support / exact evidence: The review notes that several early textile claims were based on impressions and later disputed; securely identified woven hemp includes Bronze Age examples such as Gaocheng Taixi.
- Limits / qualification: Cord-impressed pottery and seed finds are still relevant evidence for plant use, but should not be described as directly preserved hemp cloth without confirmation.

### C11
- Claim: Direct chemical evidence shows psychoactive cannabis was burned in wooden braziers in the eastern Pamirs around 500 BCE.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Ren et al., Science Advances (2019), doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw1391; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561734/
- Support / exact evidence: Chemical residues from Jirzankal Cemetery braziers contained cannabinoid markers consistent with cannabis possessing high psychoactive compound levels.
- Limits / qualification: The authors frame this as some of the earliest directly dated and scientifically verified evidence for ritual cannabis smoking/burning, not the first psychoactive use ever.

### C12
- Claim: The Pamir evidence does not by itself prove that people had already bred a standardized high-THC drug cultivar.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Ren et al. (2019).
- Support / exact evidence: Authors discuss multiple possibilities, including deliberate selection of stronger plants, naturally high-THC populations, or hybridization/domestication effects.
- Limits / qualification: Narration must preserve these alternatives.

### C13
- Claim: Cannabis’ natural center of origin and its domestication center are not necessarily the same question.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Reviews: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10485653/ and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9104644/
- Support / exact evidence: Reviews summarize competing hypotheses for deeper natural origin (Central Asia, northeastern Tibetan Plateau, low-latitude Asia) while distinguishing them from the 2021 East Asian domestication model.
- Limits / qualification: This distinction should be explicit to avoid false certainty.

### C14
- Claim: Cannabis’ long history of cultivation and easy escape from cultivation make “wild” versus “feral” difficult to distinguish.
- Status: SUPPORTED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021); archaeobotanical review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127845/
- Support / exact evidence: Genomic clustering of feral samples with cultivated groups plus observations that escaped plants can rapidly regain wild-type traits.
- Limits / qualification: Do not claim every wild-growing plant is definitely feral.

### C15
- Claim: Cannabis spread widely because one species offered several valuable products: bast fiber, edible/oil-rich seeds, and psychoactive/medicinal chemistry.
- Status: ESTABLISHED
- Source: Ren et al. (2021); broad reviews.
- Support / exact evidence: Multiple independent literatures document these end uses and their association with differentiated cultivars.
- Limits / qualification: Specific uses varied strongly by region and period.

### C16
- Claim: The global history of cannabis is better understood as repeated human selection and movement than as a single moment when the plant was “domesticated.”
- Status: INTERPRETATION
- Source: Synthesis of genomic and archaeological evidence above.
- Support / exact evidence: Domestication date estimates, later divergence, regional dispersal, and continuing breeding all indicate a long process.
- Limits / qualification: This is the documentary thesis, not a directly measured fact.

## 4. Strong cases / objects / studies

### Case A — 110-genome domestication reconstruction (2021)
- Shows: East Asian early-Neolithic domestication model, basal Chinese gene pool, later hemp/drug divergence.
- Evidence: Whole-genome resequencing and demographic/phylogenetic analyses.
- Story use: Backbone for origin and transformation.

### Case B — Haimenkou cannabis seeds, Yunnan
- Shows: Archaeobotanical populations can reveal a crop in transition toward specialized uses.
- Evidence: >800 archaeological grains measured and compared morphometrically with modern material.
- Story use: Concrete bridge from genetics to what farmers physically selected and planted.

### Case C — Gaocheng Taixi woven hemp
- Shows: Secure physical evidence for developed hemp-fiber technology in Bronze Age China.
- Evidence: Archaeological textile identified as hemp, discussed in recent archaeobotanical synthesis.
- Story use: Fiber-specialization branch.

### Case D — Jirzankal wooden braziers, eastern Pamirs
- Shows: Directly dated chemical evidence of psychoactive cannabis burning around 500 BCE.
- Evidence: GC-MS chemical residue analysis from mortuary braziers.
- Story use: Drug/ritual branch without overclaiming when psychoactive use began.

### Case E — Cannabinoid synthase divergence
- Shows: Selection left a molecular signature in pathways underlying chemical phenotype.
- Evidence: Selection scans and gene-function differences in 2021 genomic study.
- Story use: Explain how human preferences became encoded in plant genomes.

### Case F — “Wild” cannabis problem
- Shows: Domestication can erase its own starting point; feral descendants can masquerade as wild ancestors.
- Evidence: Genomic placement of feral accessions and unresolved wild-progenitor debate.
- Story use: Mystery/epistemic tension near opening or origin section.

## 6. Important uncertainties

1. Natural origin vs domestication center: deeper biogeographic origin remains debated even if the 2021 genomic dataset supports East Asian domestication.
2. “12,000 years ago” is a model estimate with a broad interval, not a dated farm or a single moment of domestication.
3. The true wild progenitor may be extinct, unsampled, or obscured by millennia of cultivation and feralization.
4. Very early fiber-use claims are uneven in quality; impressions or associated artifacts should not automatically be called hemp textiles.
5. Ancient seed size can suggest cultivation and specialization but cannot perfectly classify end use.
6. Jirzankal proves psychoactive cannabis burning by ca. 500 BCE, not the first psychoactive use in human history.
7. High psychoactive chemistry at Jirzankal could reflect deliberate selection, naturally potent populations, or hybridization; the exact pathway is unresolved.
8. “Hemp” and “marijuana/drug type” are useful modern shorthand but can flatten historical diversity.

## Readiness

Gate A: PASS.

The central question can be answered without relying on UNVERIFIED claims. The research supports a causal transformation: early multipurpose cultivation → repeated human selection → divergence into specialized fiber/seed and drug-associated forms → movement and continued breeding. The largest uncertainty to preserve is the difference between a genomic domestication model and the still-debated deeper natural origin of Cannabis.
