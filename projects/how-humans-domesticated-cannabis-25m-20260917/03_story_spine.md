# Story Spine — How Did Humans Domesticate Cannabis?

## 1. Selected hook

- Selected ID: H4
- Mechanism: Reverse assumption
- Hook text: “Cannabis was probably not domesticated because ancient people wanted a stronger drug. The evidence points to something broader: an early crop valuable for several things at once, including fiber and seed, with highly specialized drug and hemp forms emerging much later. What looks today like one plant with one famous use began as a much messier relationship between humans and a remarkably versatile species.”
- Why it fits: Modern cannabis is culturally dominated by its psychoactive identity, so correcting that assumption creates a natural route into domestication as a long, multipurpose human-plant relationship rather than a single discovery of intoxication.

## 2. Central question

How did repeated human cultivation, selection, and movement turn an early multipurpose cannabis population into the highly specialized fiber, seed, and psychoactive forms we recognize today?

## 3. Story spine

### Beat 1 — The wrong starting point: cannabis before “marijuana” and “hemp”

**What happened / what do we learn?**
The documentary opens by challenging the idea that people domesticated cannabis primarily to make it more intoxicating. The strongest current synthesis instead points to an early multipurpose crop: a plant useful for seeds, fiber, and possibly medicinal or ritual properties before strongly specialized hemp- and drug-type lineages emerged.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
It changes the problem from “who invented psychoactive cannabis?” to “how did one versatile crop get pulled in different biological directions by different human needs?”

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
If domestication was broad and ancient, we need to know where and when that relationship began—and why the answer is hard to reconstruct.

**Evidence / cases used:** C1, C5, C15, C16; Case A.

### Beat 2 — Reconstructing a beginning that may no longer exist

**What happened / what do we learn?**
A 2021 whole-genome study of 110 accessions modeled early domestication in East Asia and placed the divergence from a basal gene pool in the early Neolithic, around 12,000 years before present. But this is a demographic estimate with a broad interval, not the date of a single farm or invention. The deeper natural geographic origin of the species remains debated, and supposedly “wild” plants can be feral descendants of cultivated populations.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
Domestication is a process that can erase its own starting conditions. Human cultivation, escape, and remixing make the wild ancestor difficult to identify, so genetics and archaeology must be read together.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
Once people repeatedly kept cannabis close, ordinary choices about which plants to harvest and replant became the engine of change.

**Evidence / cases used:** C2, C3, C4, C13, C14; Case A; Case F.

### Beat 3 — Utility creates the relationship

**What happened / what do we learn?**
Early cannabis offered several kinds of value from the same species. Stems could supply bast fiber; seeds could provide food and oil; chemical properties could matter medically, ritually, or psychoactively. Archaeological evidence is uneven, and some famous very early “hemp textile” claims are weaker than popular retellings suggest, so the story uses secure seed and textile evidence rather than overclaiming.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
A multipurpose plant gives humans a reason to cultivate it across many generations and environments. That persistence creates repeated opportunities for selection.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
Cultivation does not require knowledge of genetics to change a genome. Replanting preferred plants turns practical choices into inherited traits.

**Evidence / cases used:** C5, C10, C15; Case C.

### Beat 4 — The invisible breeding program

**What happened / what do we learn?**
Farmers selecting plants for practical properties—longer useful stems, larger or more desirable seeds, architecture suited to dense fiber plots, or stronger chemical effects—change which variants reproduce. Some selection is deliberate; some is an indirect consequence of cultivation. The 2021 genomic study found differentiation associated with branching and cellulose/lignin biology, as well as cannabinoid-synthase pathways.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
This is the causal engine of domestication: no ancient farmer needs to understand DNA. Repeated differential reproduction is enough to move populations.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
When different communities repeatedly favor different end products, a generalist crop can begin to split into specialized trajectories.

**Evidence / cases used:** C6, C7, C8; Case E.

### Beat 5 — One crop starts pulling apart

**What happened / what do we learn?**
The genomic reconstruction proposes that early domesticated cannabis remained largely multipurpose until roughly 4,000 years ago, after which lineages associated with fiber production and drug production show strong divergent selection. Tall, less-branched fiber forms and resin/chemistry-focused drug forms represent different solutions to different human goals, though historical populations existed on a continuum rather than in two perfectly clean boxes.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
This is the transformation point: domestication is no longer just keeping cannabis near people; improvement begins to create biologically distinct forms.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
Genomes show the divergence statistically, but archaeological seeds and textiles let us see pieces of that diversification in physical objects.

**Evidence / cases used:** C5, C6, C7, C8; Case A; Case E.

### Beat 6 — Seeds and cloth: the crop becomes visible in archaeology

**What happened / what do we learn?**
At Haimenkou in southwest China, more than 800 archaeological cannabis grains were recovered. Morphometric analysis places much of the assemblage in overlapping ranges rather than a single clean modern category, which the researchers interpret as consistent with a crop diversifying toward alternative uses. Secure woven hemp from Gaocheng Taixi provides a concrete Bronze Age example of developed fiber use.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
The evidence shows why domestication cannot be reconstructed from modern labels alone. Intermediate and overlapping forms are exactly what a long diversification process should produce.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
Fiber and seed are only part of the story. By the first millennium BCE, archaeology also provides direct chemical evidence for psychoactive cannabis use.

**Evidence / cases used:** C9, C10; Case B; Case C.

### Beat 7 — The psychoactive branch becomes unmistakable

**What happened / what do we learn?**
Around 500 BCE at Jirzankal Cemetery in the eastern Pamirs, wooden braziers preserved cannabinoid residues consistent with cannabis containing high levels of psychoactive compounds. This is directly dated, scientifically verified evidence of cannabis burning in a mortuary context. It does not prove the first psychoactive use, nor does it prove a standardized high-THC cultivar; stronger chemistry could reflect deliberate selection, naturally potent local populations, or hybridization.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
It demonstrates that by this point at least some people were using cannabis in a way where psychoactive chemistry mattered, but it also shows that this branch appears within a much older domestication story rather than defining its beginning.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
Once specialized forms existed, human movement spread them, while escape and interbreeding kept blurring the boundary between cultivated and wild-growing cannabis.

**Evidence / cases used:** C11, C12; Case D.

### Beat 8 — Humans spread the plant, and the plant kept escaping our categories

**What happened / what do we learn?**
Cannabis moved widely with people across Eurasia and later much farther. Different communities carried different landraces and priorities. Cultivated plants escaped, feralized, crossed with other populations, and sometimes reacquired wild-type traits. Later breeding intensified the differences, producing modern industrial hemp, oilseed forms, and chemically specialized drug cultivars.

**Why does it matter to the central question?**
Domestication was never a single switch. It was repeated selection plus movement, followed by continued mixing and specialization.

**What does it naturally lead to next?**
The ending can now answer the central question directly without pretending there was one inventor, one place, or one motive.

**Evidence / cases used:** C6, C14, C15, C16; Case F.

## 4. Evidence placement

- 2021 110-genome study: Beats 1–2 and 5, as the genomic backbone rather than a long methodology digression.
- Wild-versus-feral problem: Beat 2 and callback in Beat 8.
- Gaocheng Taixi woven hemp: Beat 3/6 as a secure concrete fiber object.
- Cannabinoid synthase divergence: Beat 4 as the molecular explanation of selection.
- Haimenkou seeds: Beat 6 as a physical intermediate/diversification case.
- Jirzankal braziers: Beat 7 as the strongest direct psychoactive-use case.

## 5. Opening bridge test

PASS.

The reverse-assumption hook flows immediately into the multipurpose-crop model. Beat 1 expands the corrected assumption instead of inserting a meta phrase such as “the answer is.” Beat 2 then asks how we can reconstruct that long transformation. The hook is therefore the beginning of the story rather than a detached trailer.

## 6. Ending

**Direct answer:** Humans domesticated cannabis by keeping a useful multipurpose plant close, repeatedly replanting and moving it, and gradually selecting different traits for different jobs. Over many generations those choices changed both plant form and chemistry, eventually producing specialized fiber-, seed-, and drug-associated lineages.

**Synthesis:** multipurpose cultivation → repeated selection → divergence of end uses → regional movement and specialization → continued escape, mixing, and breeding.

**Callback:** The modern plant famous for psychoactive chemistry is not the reason the whole story began; it is one highly specialized result of a much older relationship between people and a plant useful in several different ways.
