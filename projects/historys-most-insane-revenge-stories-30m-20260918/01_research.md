# Research

## Central question candidates

1. How did acts of revenge turn private injury into consequences large enough to reshape cities, regimes, and empires?
2. Which of history's most famous revenge stories survive serious source-checking, and which became more extreme as chroniclers retold them?

## Timeline / causal background

- **6th century BCE — Media/Persia:** Herodotus tells of Harpagus, whose son was killed and served to him by King Astyages; years later Harpagus allegedly helped Cyrus revolt against Astyages. Herodotus himself frames parts of the Cyrus cycle as story/legend, so the revenge mechanism is usable only with attribution.
- **6th century BCE — Massagetae/Persia:** Herodotus says Queen Tomyris fought Cyrus after her son was captured and died, and tells the famous story of her placing Cyrus's severed head in blood. He explicitly notes that there were multiple stories of Cyrus's death.
- **Early 1st century BCE — Mediterranean:** Plutarch and Suetonius recount that Julius Caesar was captured by pirates, ransomed, then raised a force, captured them, and had them crucified; Suetonius adds that he ordered their throats cut first.
- **AD 60–61 — Roman Britain:** Tacitus reports that after the death of Prasutagus, Roman officials seized property, Boudica was flogged, and her daughters were assaulted. The Iceni and allies then revolted and destroyed major Roman settlements before the rebellion was defeated.
- **AD 695–711 — Byzantine Empire:** Justinian II was overthrown and mutilated in 695, returned to power in 705 with outside military support, and had his predecessors Leontios and Tiberios publicly humiliated and executed. Later sources also describe his second reign as marked by revenge against perceived enemies.
- **AD 945–946 — Kyivan Rus':** The Primary Chronicle says Igor was killed by the Drevlians and then narrates Olga's escalating revenge, including burned envoys, a funeral massacre, and a bird-based burning of Iskorosten. The chronicle was compiled generations later and scholars identify clear legendary/folkloric features.
- **AD 1218–1221 — Central Asia:** At Otrar, a Mongol-linked merchant caravan was massacred; a later embassy seeking redress was rejected, with an ambassador killed according to major sources. The episode helped precipitate Genghis Khan's invasion of the Khwarazmian Empire. This was geopolitical retaliation as well as strategy, not merely personal rage.

## Useful numbers and quotes

- **Target script length:** 4,740 words at 158 WPM.
- **Caesar:** Plutarch says the captivity lasted **38 days** and that the pirates initially demanded **20 talents**, while Caesar told them to demand **50**. Treat these as Plutarch's details, not independently verified numbers.
- **Boudica:** Revolt commonly dated **AD 60–61**; avoid ancient casualty totals unless independently audited because ancient war numbers are often inflated.
- **Olga:** The Primary Chronicle says the first embassy contained **20 men** and later describes a demand for **three pigeons and three sparrows from each household**. These are chronicle details, not secure factual measurements.
- **Justinian II:** first reign ended **695**; restored **705**; killed **711**.
- **Otrar:** Iranica reports **450 merchants** in the caravan and a siege of about **five months**.
- **Short source-language anchor, Herodotus on Harpagus's motive:** "wishing to be avenged on Astyages" (use only if a direct quote is genuinely helpful).
- **Short source-language anchor, Tacitus on the revolt trigger:** the Iceni were "roused by these insults" in the older translation. Better to paraphrase in narration.
- **No long ancient speeches should be quoted verbatim.** Tacitus's Boudica speech is literary composition, not a transcript.

## Important uncertainties

1. **Harpagus:** The banquet and hare-message conspiracy come from Herodotus, writing long after the fall of Astyages. The political transition is historical; the revenge choreography is not independently verified.
2. **Tomyris:** Herodotus explicitly acknowledges multiple accounts of Cyrus's death. The blood-filled skin episode must be attributed to Herodotus and cannot be presented as certain.
3. **Caesar and the pirates:** Plutarch and Suetonius preserve a consistent core anecdote, but both are later biographers and use the story to illustrate Caesar's character. Exact dialogue, ransom behavior, and timing should stay attributed.
4. **Boudica:** Her revolt had multiple causes—political annexation, dispossession, enslavement, debt/financial pressure in other sources, and the reported abuse of her family. A "she did it all for revenge" thesis would be misleading.
5. **Olga:** The Primary Chronicle is much later than the events; the four-part revenge cycle has folkloric structure, and the incendiary-bird stratagem is specifically flagged by scholarship as legendary. The safest documentary move is to make that uncertainty part of the story.
6. **Justinian II:** His mutilation, restoration, and executions are well attested; generalized claims that he spent his whole second reign purely "getting revenge" should be attributed and narrowed to documented acts.
7. **Otrar/Genghis Khan:** The merchant massacre and diplomatic rupture are strongly sourced, but the subsequent invasion also served strategic, imperial, and military objectives. It is too simple to call the entire war an emotional revenge campaign.
8. **Title framing:** "Most insane" is subjective. The narration should say these are "some of history's most extreme revenge stories" rather than claim a provable ranking.
